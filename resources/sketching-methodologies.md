# Sketching Learning Methodologies

How beginners are actually taught to draw — distilled from teaching sites and
tutorials — and how each idea maps into **Sketch on Your Own**.

---

## 1. The Grid Method

The oldest and most reliable way to copy a reference accurately. You overlay a
rectangular grid on the reference, draw the same grid (lightly) on your paper,
then copy **one cell at a time**. Because each cell is small and simple, you stop
trying to draw "a face" and instead just draw the handful of lines inside a
square — which is far easier and far more accurate.

**Why it works:** it breaks an overwhelming whole into small, manageable pieces,
and it forces you to observe *relationships* (where a line enters and exits a
cell) rather than draw from memory.

**In the app:** the grid is the backbone. Columns are lettered (A, B, C…) and
rows numbered (1, 2, 3…) exactly like a classic artist's grid, and every cell has
its own instruction.

---

## 2. The Loomis Method (portrait proportions)

A construction method for heads. You build the head from simple forms and
**measured landmarks** rather than copying outlines blindly:

- The cranium is drawn as a **ball/sphere**; the jaw is added below it.
- A **central axis line** splits the face into symmetric halves.
- Horizontal **landmark lines** divide the face: brow line, eye line, nose line,
  mouth line.

**The single most useful beginner fact:** *the eyes sit roughly halfway down the
whole head*, not near the top — beginners almost always place features too high.
Other classic proportions:

- Eyes are about **one eye-width apart**.
- The **bottom of the nose** sits about halfway between the eye line and the chin.
- The **mouth** sits about one-third of the way from the nose to the chin.
- Ears run roughly from the **brow line to the bottom of the nose**.

**In the app:** when a face is detected, we draw Loomis-style **proportion guide
lines** (center axis + brow/eye/nose/mouth lines) over the face box and explain
these landmarks, so the learner places features correctly instead of guessing.

---

## 3. Block-In First (big shapes before detail)

Skilled artists almost never start with details. They start with a **block-in**:
a light, rough sketch of the whole composition's *major shapes and proportions*,
to make sure everything is placed correctly and fits on the page. Only once the
big shapes are right do they add smaller forms and, last of all, detail and
shading.

The mantra: **"Draw the big shapes first, then the medium shapes, then the small
shapes."** Use the big shapes as a map to place the features, and the features as
a map to place the fine details.

**In the app:** the first drawing phase is a **block-in** — we show only the
strongest, largest contours (a bold, simplified outline) so the learner lays down
the overall structure before touching any detail.

---

## 4. Construction Lines

Light guidelines that preserve basic shapes and proportions. Typical practice:
draw a single **vertical and horizontal axis** through the middle of both the
reference and your paper, then gauge every shape's size and position relative to
those axes and to the grid. These lines are drawn lightly and erased later.

**In the app:** the grid lines *are* the construction scaffold, plus the facial
proportion guides for portraits. Everything is drawn faintly and the final step
tells you to erase it.

---

## 5. Shading Stages (value, done last)

Shading turns a flat outline into something that reads as three-dimensional, and
it comes **after** the line drawing is correct. The staged process:

1. **Find the extremes first** — locate the **darkest darks** and the
   **lightest lights** by squinting at the reference (squinting collapses detail
   into simple value shapes).
2. **Build tonal values** — work from light to dark in a few steps rather than
   trying to nail the final tone immediately.
3. **Use a shading technique** — **hatching** (parallel lines) and
   **cross-hatching** for buildable, controllable tone; **stippling** (dots);
   or smooth **gradation** by varying pencil pressure / blending.

**In the app:** shading is its own final phase. We quantise the photo into a few
value bands, highlight the darkest regions, and animate **hatching** into them,
with instructions to squint and work light-to-dark.
